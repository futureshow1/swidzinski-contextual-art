Introduction

In the summer of 1976, a sixty-page brochure appeared at an experimental gallery in the Swedish university town of Lund. Its author was a fifty-three-year-old Polish artist named Jan Świdziński. Its title was disarmingly direct: Art as Contextual Art. Inside, twelve numbered theses laid out a systematic critique of conceptualism and proposed a radical alternative — an art that would abandon the search for universals and instead embed itself entirely within the specific coordinates of time, place, situation, and persons.

The brochure did not arrive into a vacuum. By the mid-1970s, conceptual art was showing signs of internal exhaustion. Joseph Kosuth, its most visible theorist, had himself begun moving away from the tautological model of "Art After Philosophy" (1969) toward an "anthropologized" approach. But Kosuth's revision remained anchored in the intellectual frameworks of the Western art world. What Świdziński proposed was something different: a theory born from the specific experience of an artist working within the socialist system of the People's Republic of Poland, drawing on the Polish school of formal logic, Slavic structuralism, and Marxian social consciousness — intellectual traditions that had little presence in the Anglo-American conceptual discourse.

The significance of contextual art lies not merely in its theoretical propositions but in the circumstances of its production and the network of relations it generated. Świdziński's manifesto was simultaneously a philosophical text, a tactical weapon, and a geopolitical statement. It challenged what its author called "hegemony in art" — the enforcement of one cultural system's "personal notions, values, and rules of appraising cultures" on others. It spoke from the periphery against the center, from the East against the West, from experience of living under an authoritarian system against the assumptions of capitalist art-world ideology. And it did so not by retreating into cultural nationalism but by proposing a universal mechanism — the pragmatic context — through which every particular artistic language could assert its legitimacy.

This study attempts a comprehensive reconstruction of the contextual art project: its intellectual origins, its theoretical architecture, its practical manifestations in performance and text, its international reception, and its contemporary relevance. It is organized in five parts. Part I reconstructs the biography, drawing on established sources as well as more recent (and not yet independently verified) archival research. Part II traces the evolution of Świdziński's thought across seven phases, from the diagnostic "Spór o istnienie sztuki" (1970) through the manifesto (1976) to the acceptance of "Jest jak jest" (1998), centering the artist's own voice throughout. Part III examines performance as the privileged medium of contextual practice. Part IV maps three distinct schools of scholarly interpretation: Kazimierz Piotrowski's nonmonotonic aesthetics, Łukasz Ronduda's art-historiographic revision, and Łukasz Guzek's documentation theory. Part V assesses contextual art's resonance with contemporary concerns — relational aesthetics, post-truth epistemology, and digital context.

The study draws on the complete corpus of Świdziński's own texts (thirteen texts spanning 1970–1998), three major interviews, eyewitness testimony from collaborators, and the critical literature produced by two generations of scholars. A key finding concerns the origin of the concept itself: the word "contextual" (kontekstualny) first appears not in the 1976 Lund manifesto but in the 1975 text "Model kina" (Cinema Model), written for the Workshop of the Film Form — demonstrating that contextual art theory emerged organically from the analysis of experimental film practice rather than from pure philosophical speculation.

Throughout, this study attempts to let Świdziński speak for himself. His theoretical writings, interviews, and public statements — often in Polish, here presented with English translation — constitute the primary evidence. The secondary literature is used to illuminate, contextualize, and sometimes challenge what the primary sources reveal. The result is, we hope, not another "collection of truths and norms" to be "treated as truth" — Bartosz Łukasiewicz's warning applies to this study as much as to the theory it examines — but a resource that enables readers to engage with contextual art on their own terms, in their own pragmatic contexts.

PART I: THE LIFE
From Bydgoszcz to Powązki (1923–2014)

1. The Officer's Son (1923–1939)

Jan Świdziński was born on 25 May 1923 in Bydgoszcz, in a Poland that had existed as an independent state for only five years. His father, also named Jan, was an officer of the Polish Army in the rank of captain — a former soldier of Józef Piłsudski's Legions who had fought for Polish independence. The family descended from the knightly Ligęza family, bearing the Półkozic coat of arms, which had arrived in the fourteenth century from the Lublin region to the southern edge of the Duchy of Mazovia. This aristocratic lineage — which Świdziński himself rarely mentioned — placed him in a social milieu where honor, military service, and national identity were defining values.

In one of his wartime battles, the elder Świdziński had received a head wound that would prove fatal: in 1938, when the artist was fifteen, his father died as a consequence of this old injury. Before his death, the major (he had been promoted upon retiring from active service) had completed economics studies and worked in the Supervisory Board of Polish Radio. The mother, Stanisława née Tkaczyk, born in 1898 in Radom, had worked at the PKP Income Control Office in Bydgoszcz before devoting herself to the household — officers' pensions for former Legionnaires being sufficiently generous.

In 1932, the family moved to Kraków, settling on Wielicka Street near the military barracks — a relocation presumably connected with the father's reassignment. In Kraków, the future artist attended elementary school until 1935, when the family moved to Warsaw. Stanisława and her son settled in Praga-Północ at Markowska 14; Captain Świdziński lived separately at Adam Pług Street in Stara Ochota. In Warsaw, Jan continued his education — by his own later reckoning, he attended approximately fifteen schools before completing three years of gymnasium.

After his father's death in 1938, Jan and his mother moved to the home of her older sister, Justyna Kolasińska, in Nowy Rembertów at Budkiewicza 17. It was there that the outbreak of war found them in September 1939.

2. Double Agent: Ballet, Kripo, and the Home Army (1939–1945)

The wartime biography of Jan Świdziński has been the subject of scholarly discussion since Zbigniew Libera's 2020 article in Przekrój, "Jan Świdziński i spór o istnienie sztuki" (from the series Libera wybiera, czyli subiektywny poczet polskich artystów) (The Contextual Gospel According to Jan). Libera, one of Poland's most important contemporary artists — who had included Świdziński in his "Mistrzowie" (Masters) series in 2004 — presented a wartime narrative based on archival materials that he interpreted as showing a more complex biography than the standard accounts. It must be noted, however, that Libera is an artist and not a trained historian, and his claims have not been independently verified by any neutral expert at the Institute of National Memory (IPN). IPN files from the Stalinist and postwar periods are notoriously complex documents requiring specialist interpretation; they were frequently fabricated, exaggerated, or maintained without the genuine cooperation of the persons named in them. What follows presents Libera's claims alongside the established biographical record, with appropriate scholarly caution.

After a year of inactivity following the outbreak of war, Świdziński began attending preparatory courses at the Warsaw gymnasium "Oświata" and enrolled in ballet school — a private studio at Mokotowska 63, where he trained under Leon Wójcikowski, known in ballet circles as the successor to Vaslav Nijinsky. In June 1941, he passed the entrance examination for the Department of Interior Architecture at the Construction Lyceum, housed at the pre-war Polytechnic campus. After a year, illness forced him to interrupt his studies.

It was around this time that the story takes its darkest turn. A former friend of his father, Captain Ejzert, recruited him into cooperation with AK (Home Army) intelligence. According to unconfirmed reports cited by Libera, Świdziński transported correspondence on the Warsaw–Kraków route. Simultaneously, during a visit to an aunt in Chełm, he received a proposal from a translator of the German Criminal Police to join Kripo (Kriminalpolizei). This proposal "reportedly met with satisfaction in Captain Ejzert's circles" — the AK intelligence intended to use young Świdziński as their mole inside the German police.

According to Libera's account (based on archival materials whose authenticity and interpretation have not been independently verified), in February 1944 Świdziński was admitted to German Security Police service in Warsaw and sent for training to the Kripo commissariat in Radzymin. Libera claims he received the rank of Criminal Police corporal. Libera himself notes that "these reports remain only in the sphere of conjecture; there is no longer anyone who could confirm them. Nor do any relevant documents exist." The absence of corroborating documentation is crucial: we are dealing with a single author's interpretation of fragmentary archival traces, published six years after the subject's death, when he could no longer respond.

If the AK intelligence dimension of this account is accurate — that the resistance used young Świdziński as a mole inside the German police — then the experience would have a profound structural resonance with the later theory. The founder of contextual art would have spent his formative years operating in a situation where the meaning of his actions was determined entirely by which context — German police or Polish resistance — was the operative frame of reference. But we must be cautious about over-interpreting unverified biographical claims as explanations for theoretical positions that have their own intellectual genealogy.

In his 2006 interview for Sztuka i Filozofia, Świdziński offered a carefully edited version of these events, acknowledging his AK membership while omitting the Kripo dimension:

"I was in the AK, not because I was brave, but because that's what everyone did — all my friends and the majority of the community… I could have gotten the death sentence, or Siberia, or five years… I chose the last option — it turned out there were only three soldiers. So what, are three of us going to fight a war? No choice. The month ended and the replenishment arrived."Jan Świdziński, Sztuka i Filozofia, nr 29, June 2006

3. The Penal Company and the Theater of War (1945)

After the Warsaw Uprising and the entry of the Red Army into Rembertów in September 1944, Świdziński — like many other young men from the AK — volunteered for the 2nd Infantry Division of the Polish Army (the Dąbrowski Division). He was assigned to the Soldier's Theater as a private-artist. Performing alongside him were Tadeusz Janczar (then still bearing the surname Musiał, later a famous film actor) and Józef Nalberczak.

Soviet intelligence, however, soon unmasked him as a former Kripo corporal. He was given a choice: "either 5 years in prison or a month in a penal company." The penal company — kompania karna — was, in Świdziński's own words:

"Such a company was sent into the worst fire, to certain death. From the earlier composition of my company, only two soldiers remained. When I joined, there were three of us. Since there was no replenishment, we had to wait for other convicts. So fortunately that month passed."Jan Świdziński, quoted in Libera, Przekrój, 2020

In the meantime, the war ended. In October 1945, Świdziński was demobilized.

Świdziński himself, in the 2006 interview, described his wartime military service in terms that — whether or not they relate to the specific circumstances Libera alleges — convey the absurdity and arbitrariness of wartime fate: "I could have gotten the death sentence, or Siberia, or five years… I chose the last option — it turned out there were only three soldiers. So what, are three of us going to fight a war?" What is certain is that the wartime generation experienced a world where the meaning of any act was determined entirely by shifting contexts — where the "pragmatic context" was not an intellectual concept but a survival condition.

4. The Academy, Socialist Realism, and "Czwartek" (1945–1959)

In 1945, Świdziński enrolled at the State Higher School of Fine Arts in Warsaw (Myśliwiecka 8), studying in the Department of Interiors and the Painting Workshop. From February 1948, he additionally attended the Academy of Fine Arts as a free listener, studying under Jan Cybis (painting), Tadeusz Kulisiewicz (graphics), and Ksawery Piwocki (art history). He also began working as an editor of the reportage section at the weekly Razem, publishing under a pseudonym. Later he wrote for Przegląd Artystyczny, where — in a detail that complicates the standard narrative of his anti-establishment stance — he propagated socialist realist art.

In 1948, he married Magdalena Spasowicz, a fellow academy student. They lived with his mother in the old Rembertów apartment. The marriage lasted until 1955; after the separation, Magdalena partnered with the painter Aleksander Winnicki, a former member of the Kraków Group. Świdziński married Irena Psarska, a graduate of the PWSP in Poznań and member of the realist painters' group "R-55." In a remarkable detail noted by Libera, "Świdziński and Psarska became witnesses at the wedding of Jan's former wife with Aleksander Winnicki."

In 1952, Świdziński graduated from the Warsaw ASP. And then came the event that Libera's article brought to light for the first time.

Libera further claims that on 7 May 1953, after being detained and subjected to more than twelve hours of interrogation by officers of Department I of the Ministry of Public Security, Świdziński signed a commitment to cooperate with the UB (Security Office) under the pseudonym "Czwartek" (Thursday). According to Libera, this cooperation continued until 1959.

These claims require substantial qualification. First, they have not been independently verified by IPN archival experts. Second, the context of Stalinist Poland in 1953 — the most repressive year of the Communist period, months after Stalin's death — makes any "commitment" signed after twelve hours of interrogation an act of coercion, not voluntary collaboration. Thousands of Polish citizens were subjected to similar pressure; the resulting documents reflect the security apparatus's methods rather than genuine biographical facts about the persons named. Third, SB files are institutional records that served the interests of the recording institution; they are primary sources for the history of the security apparatus, not transparent windows onto the lives of those surveilled. Fourth, Libera himself acknowledges the speculative nature of some claims: the Kripo allegations "remain only in the sphere of conjecture."

What is not in dispute is that the postwar decades were a period of extraordinary pressure on Polish intellectuals and artists, and that many — including figures now celebrated as dissidents — navigated this pressure through various forms of accommodation that do not lend themselves to simple moral judgment. Świdziński never spoke publicly about these matters.

The scholarly question is whether and how these biographical circumstances relate to the theoretical framework of contextual art. The theory that meaning is determined by pragmatic context, and that "sufficient authority" can enforce its interpretation (Thesis 2), resonates with the experience of living under a system that claimed the power to determine who people "really were" through institutional records. But drawing direct causal lines between unverified biographical claims and theoretical positions that have their own rich intellectual genealogy — in Polish logic, semiotics, Wittgenstein, and Marxian critique — risks both over-simplification and unfairness to a person who cannot respond.

5. The Failed Painter (1959–1969)

In 1959, Świdziński's situation finally improved. The security services disengaged. He received an assignment to a studio-apartment on the top floor of a new building at Waryńskiego 14a, in central Warsaw — the address that would become the meeting point for generations of Polish artists and intellectuals. His daughter was born.

The problem was his art. After the collapse of socialist realism, Świdziński turned first to the colorist tradition, then to formalist monochromatic abstractions. Neither convinced. In 1965, the critic Ignacy Witz published a devastating assessment in Życie Warszawy:

"His painting seems to me so inexpressive, so impersonal, so technically feeble, that searching here for positives by force would not testify to my sincerity or my truthfulness, to which I am obligated toward my readers."Ignacy Witz, Życie Warszawy, 1965

As Libera observes: "Rejected by criticism, and presumably also conscious of his own weakness in the field of painting, Świdziński began to seek power in knowledge." Between 1960 and 1965, he undertook independent systematic studies in formal logic, semiotics, linguistics, and structuralism, concentrating on problems of communication and information. Thanks to his childhood French — he had been learning the language since early youth — he could use the richly stocked library of the Institut Français in Warsaw, where he acquainted himself with French Theory and the latest developments in world art.

This is a crucial biographical moment. The transition from failed painter to successful theorist was not a natural evolution but a compensatory strategy — a deliberate reorientation of intellectual energy from a domain of weakness (visual art production) to a domain of strength (logical analysis and theoretical writing). The "empty sign" — the artwork that carries no inherent meaning but must be "filled" by its context — may be read, at the biographical level, as the theoretical sublimation of the experience of artistic emptiness: the painter whose work was "inexpressive" and "impersonal" transformed that very emptiness into a theoretical virtue.

6. Logic, Semiotics, and the Institut Français (1960–1970)

Between 1965 and 1970, Świdziński headed the Art Research Department at the Polish Cybernetics Society, working alongside Marek Konieczny in the architecture and design section. This position placed him at the intersection of art and systems thinking — of aesthetics and formal logic, of sign theory and feedback mechanisms.

The cybernetics connection was more significant than it might appear. Cybernetics — the study of communication and control in systems — provided Świdziński with a vocabulary for thinking about art as a system of meaning-production rather than as a collection of individual objects. The concept of feedback, central to cybernetic theory, would appear explicitly in Thesis 3 of the 1976 manifesto: "The reality that we shape depends on our concepts of reality, our concepts of reality depend on the reality that we create."

His most significant artistic works from this period were the "Semantic Signs" (Znaki semantyczne) paintings — canvases that treated the painting surface as a field of signs whose meanings were not inherent but dependent on structural relationships. These works served as a bridge between his formal art training and his emerging semiotic interests.

In his 2006 interview, Świdziński recalled the intellectual atmosphere of this period:

"I was very taken with structuralism at one point, but that's understandable, it was the 1960s, everything was under the sign of this current. But Habermas himself, who is younger than me, would no longer call me a structuralist — though he'd agree with me that he too operates within this framework."Świdziński, Sztuka i Filozofia, 2006

And on his engagement with analytic philosophy:

"Kosuth himself based his work on specific books by Ayer, copying from them literally entire pages. It is not about borrowing someone else's reasoning, since an artist has the right to do so, but about the fact of the emergence of paralogisms and paradoxes associated with it. Because saying that art is a definition of art is such a paralogism — since a definition is an attempt at description, one cannot describe something that does not exist."Świdziński, Sztuka i Filozofia, 2006

7. The Conceptual Turn: Galleries, Networks, Performance (1970–1974)

In 1970, the article "Spór o istnienie sztuki" (Controversy on the Existence of Art) appeared in Życie i Myśl — the text that, according to Kazimierz Piotrowski, initiated the conceptual turn in Polish art. In 1971, Świdziński delivered a lecture on conceptualism at Galeria Współczesna in Warsaw, run by Janusz Bogucki. That same year, he participated in the Festival of Art Schools in Nowa Ruda, where he met his future collaborators: Anna and Romuald Kutera, Przemysław Kwiek and Zofia Kulik, Jan Stanisław Wojciechowski, and the artists of the Workshop of the Film Form.

The Cybernetics Connection

The cybernetics connection was more significant than it might appear. Cybernetics — the study of communication and control in systems — provided Świdziński with a vocabulary for thinking about art as a system of meaning-production. The concept of feedback, central to cybernetic theory, would appear explicitly in Thesis 3: "The reality that we shape depends on our concepts of reality, our concepts of reality depend on the reality that we create." This is a cybernetic loop — output feeds back into input, producing a dynamic, self-modifying process.

The cybernetic perspective also informed the "outdating of meanings" as an inherent property of complex adaptive systems. A sign system that cannot update its representations in response to environmental change will lose coherence — the process described in Thesis 4. Contextual art is a mechanism for keeping the art system responsive to change — a semiotic homeostasis.

The Intellectual Sources: Tarski, Wittgenstein, Bridgman, Rorty

Three intellectual traditions fed the theory (as Ronduda identified): Marxian consciousness, the Polish school of logic (Lwów-Warsaw School — Twardowski, Łukasiewicz, Tarski), and Slavic structuralism (Jakobson, Prague Circle, Moscow-Tartu School). Tarski's semantic truth provided the framework; Świdziński's Thesis 5 uses it but arrives at an opposite conclusion: where Tarski established conditions for truth, Świdziński established conditions for truth's impermanence.

The late Wittgenstein ("the meaning is the use") provided the pragmatist warrant. Świdziński also drew on Bridgman's operational theory of meaning (1927) — art's veracity as relative to specific social operations. His late theory converges with Rorty's neopragmatism: both rejected philosophy's pretension to "mirror" reality, proposing knowledge as conversational social practice. As Świdziński stated: "My pragmatism is in a certain sense common sense."

In 1972–73, he became advisor to Gallery Zero and co-founded Mała Galeria with Zbigniew Dłubak. These institutional involvements were not peripheral activities but integral to the developing theory. Świdziński understood that art's meaning was produced not in the studio alone but in the network of relationships between artists, institutions, audiences, and critical discourses — the network that he would theorize as the "pragmatic context."

His relationship with Dłubak was particularly important. Świdziński regarded Dłubak as one of the most significant artists for his theory. With Dłubak's consent, he appropriated the "Gestykulacje" (Gesticulations) series, reinterpreting them as paradigmatic examples of contextual "empty signs." Dłubak photographed Świdziński in his studio in 1975, testifying to their close intellectual partnership.

In 1974, Świdziński began making performance art and formulated his initial theses on contextual art. He also began work on the project O logice niekompletnych rzeczywistości (On the Logic of Incomplete Realities), which, in his own words, "led him to establish the program of 'art as contextual art.'"

Art Under Surveillance

Ronduda's research in IPN archives revealed that the Secret Police interpreted conceptual art as "pro-Western" and potentially threatening. This surveillance created a peculiar double bind: works too obscure for mass audiences yet too sophisticated to dismiss. The authorities' attention confirmed, perversely, the political significance Świdziński claimed for his practice — demonstrating that a challenge to meaning-making was implicitly a challenge to power.

The relationship between alternative galleries and the state was neither purely oppositional nor collaborative. It was contextual: an exhibition tolerable under one set of conditions could become provocative under another. This endemic contextuality of artistic practice under socialism was, for Świdziński, not merely a constraint but a laboratory — a daily demonstration that meaning is never context-free.

Meanwhile, in 1973, in a characteristically eclectic aside, Świdziński supervised — together with Konieczny and his wife Irena Psarska — a project for the placement of information in the Warsaw metro planned by Edward Gierek's administration. The metro, of course, was not built for another two decades.

8. Emigration, Canada, and Return (1980–1999)

The 1980s brought upheaval. Martial law in December 1981, the suppression of Solidarity, the slow disintegration of the Communist system. Świdziński emigrated but maintained an open Canadian visa, spending extended periods in Canada and the United States. He lectured at the École Nationale Supérieure des Beaux Arts in Paris, the Université du Québec à Montréal, New York University, and universities in Calgary and Vancouver.

In Canada, he established a lasting partnership with Richard Martel and Le Lieu, Centre en art actuel in Quebec City — a center that, as multiple sources confirm, "began to function in accordance with his postulates of contextual art." Three of his most important books were published by Martel's Éditions Intervention: Art contextuel (1997), L'Art et son Contexte (2005), and the posthumous In Context (2015).

He visited the United States twelve times, staying several months each time. In New York, he befriended Joseph Kosuth, continuing the dialogue that had begun in Toronto in 1976. Anna Kutera recalled the aftermath of the Toronto conference: "Joseph Kosuth invited both of us to his place in New York and said, 'Please, take it, I'm opening the gate to New York for you.'"

In 1989, he returned to Poland — an event marked by the performance "Jestem z powrotem" (I'm Back) at Mała Galeria on 4 April 1989, just weeks before the Round Table Agreements that would end Communist rule. The title was biographical, but the meaning was contextual: the same person returning to a radically different country.

9. INTERAKCJE, Recognition, and Death (1999–2014)

From 1999, Świdziński served as President and Curator of the International Festival of the Art of Action INTERAKCJE in Piotrków Trybunalski, together with Ryszard Piegza. The choice of a provincial city was itself a contextual statement — a demonstration that meaningful artistic exchange could occur outside established cultural centers.

In May 2005, he was awarded the Officer's Cross of the Order of Polonia Restituta. In his 2006 interview, he noted the irony with characteristic dryness: "I was extremely pleased that this was noticed and at this point officially recognized and rewarded."

In 2008, he met Kosuth for the last time, in Rome — an encounter filmed by Piotr Weychert as Kosuth – Świdziński. Spotkanie w Rzymie. In 2009, two of his books were finally published in Polish translation: Sztuka i jej kontekst and Sztuka, społeczeństwo, samoświadomość (the latter translated by Łukasz Guzek). The same year, the retrospective "Jest jak jest" (It Is What It Is) at ODA Gallery in Piotrków Trybunalski, curated by Guzek, surveyed thirty-three years of contextual art practice.

Jan Świdziński died on 9 February 2014 in Warsaw, aged ninety. He was buried at the Powązki Military Cemetery, section D, row 1, grave 29.

PART II: THE THEORY
Seven Phases of Thought

The evolution of Świdziński's thinking can be traced through thirteen original texts spanning twenty-eight years (1970–1998), three major interviews, and six books. What follows is a reconstruction of this evolution based on close reading of the complete primary source corpus — the texts themselves, in Świdziński's own words.

The Grand Arc

1970: DIAGNOSIS — Art is transforming, not dying
1972: TOOL-BUILDING — Semiotics of the sign
1975: DISCOVERY — "Contextual existence" emerges from film analysis
1976: MANIFESTO — 12 theses; empty sign; O-m-t-p-s-x formula
1977–79: EXPANSION — International conferences; intentional functors

1995–2014: ACCEPTANCE — "Jest jak jest" / neo-pragmatic tourist

10. Phase 1: The Diagnosis — "Spór o istnienie sztuki" (1970)

The first phase is purely diagnostic. In "Spór o istnienie sztuki," published in Życie i Myśl (no. 5, 1970, pp. 98–105), Świdziński surveys the landscape of contemporary art and identifies a fundamental transformation — not a crisis, as Western critics insist, but a structural shift in the relationship between art, language, and reality.

The text opens with an epigraph from a review of a conceptual art exhibition: "After this, neither museums nor galleries will be possible, and yet works will remain" (Jose Pierre, L'Oeil, May 1969). The epigraph frames the central paradox: art institutions are declared obsolete, but art continues to be produced. What has changed is not art's existence but the framework within which it is recognized.

Świdziński identifies the root cause: Paris has lost its status as the art world's center. The postwar weakening of France, America's rising significance, and the emergence of local schools signal a tectonic shift. But this geopolitical analysis — center losing its grip, peripheries asserting autonomy — is more than art criticism. It prefigures the entire "Third Front" strategy of 1977, and it grows directly from the experience of a Polish artist who has always worked from the periphery.

The key insight of the text is that what looks like decline is actually transformation: "Rather than art's death, contemporary practice demonstrates continuous transformation alongside civilization itself. Documentation, conceptualization, and attitudinal positioning have supplanted traditional material focus, reflecting genuine responses to contemporary conditions rather than aesthetic degradation."

In the 2006 interview, Świdziński recalled the impact of this text:

"I wrote a short article in the 1970s and, as it turned out, it made a very big impression. It was called 'Controversy on the Existence of Art.' It resonated vividly, especially among young people, with whom it quickly became associated."Świdziński, Sztuka i Filozofia, 2006

But the text is diagnostic, not prescriptive. It identifies the problem — the severing of connections between words and objects, signs and referents — without proposing a solution. The solution would require two more years of semiotic tool-building and a decisive encounter with experimental film.

11. Phase 2: The Semiotic Toolbox — "O znaku" and "Permafo" (1972)

In 1972, Świdziński published two texts that assembled the conceptual apparatus he would later deploy in the manifesto. "O znaku" (On the Sign), published in Fotografia (June 1972), is a systematic analysis of sign theory — drawing on Saussure (signifiant/signifié), Husserl (phenomenological signs), Foucault (discursive formations), and the Prague Linguistic Circle.

The text's most important sentence anticipates the entire contextual project: "Dzieło sztuki jest możliwością, daną odbiorcy, a nie jednoznacznym stanem" — "Art constitutes possibility granted to viewers, not singular determination." This single sentence, buried in a technical semiotic analysis, contains the seed of the "empty sign" concept that would become contextual art's central innovation four years later.

The companion text, "A propos N.S. galerii Permafo" (also in Fotografia, May 1972), offers a more polemical formulation: "Only new situations constitute art situations. The rest belongs to history or archaeology." Here the diagnostic mode of "Spór" has hardened into a programmatic stance: art is not merely transforming; it must transform, because only transformation produces genuine art situations.

12. Phase 3: The Discovery — "Model kina" and the Three Models (1975)

The breakthrough came not from philosophy but from film. In 1975, at the request of members of the Workshop of the Film Form — the Łódź-based collective of experimental filmmakers including Józef Robakowski, Wojciech Bruszewski, Ryszard Waśko, and Paweł Kwiek — Świdziński wrote "Model kina" (Cinema Model), published in Zeszyt WFF no. 7.

This text, described by the UAP Poznań exhibition proposal as "perhaps one of the most important articles about experimental film in Poland," constitutes the first documented appearance of the word "kontekstualny" (contextual) in Świdziński's writings — predating the Lund manifesto by one full year.

The text describes three models of art, mapped onto three models of civilization:

  1. The Universal Model — characterized by fixed morphology and absolute time. Art operates within established genres and semantic rules. This is "the 'great cinema' of Rossellini, Antonioni." Time is a scalar that arranges facts in sequence.
  2. The Relativistic Model — "There are no absolute laws. Truth is relativized to the frame of reference, as in Einsteinian physics." Each reference system has its own space and time. Art can become "a model of itself." This is the domain of structural film — Sharits's "meaningless syntax."
  3. The Contextual Model — and here is the sentence that changes everything:
"Jedne i drugie istnieją kontekstualnie. Istnienie kontekstualne jest wyznacznikiem trzeciego modelu sztuki; przeciwstawia się modelowi relatywistycznemu, który jest skonstruowany jako coś niezależnego wobec obiektywnego modelu sztuki modernizmu lat 50. i 60. Jest to obecne stanowisko sztuki." ["Both [subjective and objective rules] exist contextually. Contextual existence is the determinant of the third model of art; it opposes the relativistic model, which is constructed as something independent of the objective model of modernist art of the 1950s and 60s. This is the present position of art."]Jan Świdziński, "Model kina," Zeszyt WFF 7, Łódź, 1975

The text also introduces two formulations that would become central to the manifesto:

"Staliśmy się artystami bez specjalności." ["We have become artists without a specialty."]Świdziński, "Model kina," 1975
"Sztuka współczesna, określając w trakcie działania własną morfologię i rozpatrywane struktury, nie ma a priori sformułowanego przepisu posługiwania się nią — jest sztuko-teorią konstruującą się poprzez praktykę artystyczną." ["Contemporary art, defining in the course of action its own morphology and examined structures, has no a priori formulated recipe for using itself — it is an art-theory constructing itself through artistic practice."]Świdziński, "Model kina," 1975

Łukasz Guzek, speaking at the TR Warszawa meeting in 2014, confirmed the genealogy: "It is from the side of this new media art, which the Film Workshop from Łódź was making, that the contextual theory actually derives. And it is the Workshop of the Film Form that took Janek, and he writes for them this important text called 'Model kina,' this is the year 1975. And thus for the first time this contextual theory is formulated."

The discovery is important not only chronologically but conceptually. It shows that contextual art did not emerge from pure philosophical speculation but from the analysis of how experimental filmmakers already worked. The "Model kina" text analyzes specific WFF works with remarkable precision:

Waśko's "Rejestracja" (1972): A street is divided into 29 segments. Camera positions are established at each point. Recording occurs in two directions. The montage juxtaposes corresponding segments. "In this way an objective (mathematical) system is created through which the relationship between image and corresponding reality is reversed." Świdziński reads this as a demonstration that "the image of reality is in every case the result of an adopted convention of structuring reality."

Robakowski's "Idę…" (1973): The camera ascends a tower's stairs while the climber counts aloud. Visual registration is "cold" (objective), audio registration is "hot" (emotional). The relationship between subject and object reverses between the two channels — demolishing the Cartesian separation of objective and subjective.

Bruszewski's "Drzwi" (1974): Doors open and close without visible intervention, accompanied by synchronized sound. "A certain logical structure of our empirical perceptions is violated. If something happens, there is a cause… Here doors close and open for no reason." The work reveals that everyday perception is not neutral observation but a semantically loaded process governed by adopted rules.

From these analyses, Świdziński derives the formula that links film to his broader theory:

"We cannot objectivize rules by throwing them outside ourselves. For we cannot abstract the rules to which we submit and those which we ourselves produce. Both exist contextually."Świdziński, "Model kina," 1975

The text concludes with a passage that reads as both a summary of WFF's achievement and a manifesto for what would become contextual art:

"Contemporary art, defining in the course of action its own morphology and examined structures, has no a priori formulated recipe for using itself — it is an art-theory constructing itself through artistic practice."Świdziński, "Model kina," 1975

Świdziński's contribution was to recognize that what the WFF filmmakers were doing in practice — creating works whose meaning depended on specific structural conditions of making and viewing — was an instance of a general principle. The "third model" of art was not his invention but his recognition: the recognition that contextual existence was already "the present position of art."

13. Phase 4: The Manifesto — Twelve Theses and the Empty Sign (1976)

In September 1975, at the F-Art Festival in Gdańsk, Świdziński met Jean Sellem, a French artist running the experimental Galerie S:t Petri in Lund, Sweden. Sellem — who, according to Libera's revelations, "was connected to the Situationist International under Guy Debord's patronage" — responded enthusiastically to Świdziński's ideas and proposed to organize an exhibition.

Anna Kutera, who was present, recalled the naming of the movement at the TR Warszawa meeting: "Jean Sellem said: 'But this needs to be named.' Since there was a great deal about context in the theoretical assumptions, Jean Sellem formulated the name of this movement and called it 'contextual art.'" The Situationist connection — Debord's theory of the spectacle and the situation — suggests an intellectual genealogy that has been insufficiently explored: Sellem may have recognized in Świdziński's contextual theory a philosophical cousin of the Situationist project.

The exhibition opened on 5 February 1976 at Galerie S:t Petri, running until 5 March. Eleven Polish artists participated: Bruszewski, Dłubak, Gajewski, Jórczak, Anna Kutera, Paweł Kwiek, Romuald Kutera, Mrożek, Robakowski, Świdziński, and Waśko. A conference was held at the Malmö Konsthall on 9 February. Five hundred copies of the manifesto brochure were printed.

The manifesto exists in multiple versions. The master text — "Sztuka kontekstualna (3)" — runs to 2,655 words and includes a formal bibliography citing Church, Hintikka, Quine, Hegel, Kosuth, Åqvist, Chomsky, Kristeva, and Malraux. This is not the work of a dilettante; it is the product of fifteen years of systematic self-education in logic, semiotics, and philosophy of language.

The manifesto exists in multiple versions. The twelve-thesis version is the most widely cited; but the master text — "Sztuka kontekstualna (3)" — runs to 2,655 words and deploys the full philosophical apparatus, with a formal bibliography citing Church, Hintikka, Quine, Hegel, Kosuth, Åqvist, Chomsky, Kristeva, and Malraux. Three concepts define the system:

The Empty Sign

In "Sztuka kontekstualna (3)," Świdziński defines the empty sign with a precision that the twelve-thesis version only approximates:

"In the contemporary situation, a sign is neither a window through which we view the world (it does not describe reality), nor is it a sign of itself containing its own idea; it is an empty sign, a place to be filled by reality."Świdziński, "Sztuka kontekstualna (3)," 1976

The definition operates through a triple negation. The sign is not a window (the representational model of pre-modern art — Malraux's Musée Imaginaire). It is not a sign of itself (the self-referential model of modernism and conceptualism — Kosuth's tautological art, Sharits's "meaningless syntax"). And it is not meaningless (it will be filled — by reality, by context, by the persons who encounter it). The empty sign is a paradoxical entity: an artwork that carries no meaning yet is not meaningless, that refuses content yet demands engagement.

Ronduda describes contextual artworks as "stimuli initiating signification processes originating from viewers rather than artists." The artist creates the conditions for meaning but does not determine the meaning itself. This is not the same as saying that "anything goes." Świdziński was emphatic: contextual art "is opposed to multi-meaning and also to relativism" (Thesis 12). The empty sign is empty not because it is meaningless but because its meaning is not predetermined — it must be determined anew in each context of reception.

The famous Świdziński quote crystallizes the idea: "For the act of drinking a glass of water to become art, it has to be performed in the right place, at the right time, and in the right company."

The Extensional/Intensional Distinction

"Sztuka kontekstualna (3)" reveals the precise logical mechanism behind Thesis 5. Świdziński identifies a fundamental distinction between two types of expression:

Extensional expressions — used by science, formal logic, and Kosuth's conceptualism. Their meaning depends solely on the denotation of their component terms. "Joseph" means the same thing regardless of who says it, where, or when. Analytical sentences operate extensionally: their truth depends on their internal logical structure, not on external circumstances.

Intensional expressions — used by contextual art. Their meaning depends on factors beyond the sentence itself. To determine what they mean, "one must refer to facts other than those that can be extracted from the idea contained in the name." Intensional expressions operate in what Hintikka calls epistemic logic — the logic of "knowing, acknowledging, rejecting, believing, supposing."

Świdziński writes in the master text:

"Contextual art rejects the condition of extensionality of expressions, which places it in opposition to contemporary formalized languages of science, to formalized systems of meanings that traditional art operated with, and also against conceptualism operating with analytical sentences."Świdziński, "Sztuka kontekstualna (3)," 1976

This is the most technically precise formulation of what separates contextual art from conceptual art. Kosuth's "art is the definition of art" is an extensional sentence — its truth depends on the internal relationship between "art" and "definition." Świdziński's "Object O assumes meaning m in time t, place p, situation s, in relation to persons x" is an intensional sentence — its truth depends on factors external to the sentence itself: the actual time, place, situation, and persons involved.

The Pragmatic Context Formula (O-m-t-p-s-x)

Thesis 5 provides the formal notation: Object "O" assumes meaning "m" in time "t," place "p," situation "s," in relation to persons "x" — then and only then. The "then and only then" (wtedy i tylko wtedy) is the language of logical biconditional: meaning obtains if and only if all specified contextual conditions are met. Change any coordinate, and the meaning changes.

The formula draws on the Polish logical tradition (Tarski's semantic definition of truth) and connects to what contemporary epistemologists call contextualism — the view that truth-conditions of knowledge attributions vary with the context of the attributor. Świdziński anticipated this philosophical development by two decades.

In the master text, he specifies the formula more precisely than in Thesis 5:

"'X' has the property of being art at time 't', in place 'm', in situation 'z', in relation to person or persons 'o' if and only if. Changing any of these elements means automatically changing the examined values. Dispute about bad and good art without providing pragmatic context is devoid of subject matter."Świdziński, "Sztuka kontekstualna (3)," 1976

The Logical Progression: Theses 1–4 and 6–11

The twelve theses move through a carefully constructed progression that deserves individual attention.

Thesis 1 establishes the inseparability of objects and meanings: "There are no objects without a meaning as there are no meanings without objects." This rejects the Kantian thing-in-itself as irrelevant to aesthetic theory. Every perceived object is already a sign. But signs are systematically multi-meaningful: one object carries different meanings in different codes, and one meaning may attach to different objects. This systematic multi-meaning is the starting condition of art — and its fundamental problem.

Thesis 2 introduces the political dimension. When different social groups attribute different meanings to the same object, the group with "sufficient authority" can impose its interpretation. This leads to the proliferation of art definitions ("everything may be art"). The thesis draws on Marxian analysis of ideology and anticipates Bourdieu's concept of cultural capital. Świdziński's unstated example is clear from his other writings: the New York art world as the group with "sufficient authority."

Thesis 3 distinguishes two modes of artistic activity: the practical (creating objects with given meanings — conventional art) and the cognitive (creating meanings for given objects — conceptual art). These exist in a feedback relation — the cybernetic loop from Świdziński's Cybernetics Society years. "The reality that we shape depends on our concepts of reality, our concepts of reality depend on the reality that we create." Conceptual art, despite its claims to critical distance, is itself a form of reality-production.

Thesis 4 introduces the "outdating of meanings" and the production of "apparent reality." Groups that have gained privileges through established meanings resist recognizing that those meanings have become invalid. The result is ideology: "apparent meanings" that maintain power structures reality has already undermined. This is the mechanism of ideological mystification derived from semiotic analysis rather than from Marx directly, giving it a formal precision that purely sociological accounts lack.

Theses 6–8 draw out implications. Thesis 6: contextual art as "a steady rejection of canons used to arrest the outdating of meanings." Thesis 7: opposition to the stabilization of both objects and meanings — leading to rejection of all definitions of art. Thesis 8 completes the Duchampian project: Duchamp mobilized the object side (any object can mean "art"); contextual art mobilizes both sides simultaneously ("each object may have the meaning of an 'art' and not be an art"). "The aim of art is the relativization of the entire area of meanings and of objects."

Thesis 9 — the most concise formulation: "Art as contextual art is concerned with the relation itself (the readiness to reup the meaning of the object in a defined pragmatic context)." This anticipates relational aesthetics by two decades.

Thesis 10 acknowledges the communicative gap: "the reality construed by the artist is something different than that which is received." This is not a failure but a structural feature — artist and audience occupy different positions within the pragmatic context.

Thesis 11 severs contextual art from science (which must "immobilize" objects by defining them) and from formal logic, placing it in the domain of "rules which become constantly outdated." This is the domain Piotrowski would later identify as nonmonotonic reasoning.

The Anti-Relativist Paradox (Thesis 12)

Thesis 12: "Art as contextual art is opposed to multi-meaning and also to relativism. One and only one expression is true in the given pragmatic context. Such expression is expressed with assertion."

This thesis has generated the most scholarly discussion because it appears to contradict the relativistic implications of the preceding theses. How can a theory that insists on the continuous "outdating of meanings" simultaneously oppose relativism?

The answer lies in the distinction between relativism across contexts and determinism within contexts. Across contexts, meaning varies — this is the contextual insight. Within any given context, meaning is singular and determinate — this is the anti-relativist commitment. Contextual art does not say "anything goes"; it says "exactly one thing goes, but which thing depends on where you are standing."

Piotrowski would later formalize this as the logical structure of nonmonotonic reasoning: conclusions that are valid given a particular set of premises but that can be overturned when new premises are added. The paradox of Thesis 12 is resolved by recognizing that contextual art operates not within a single logical framework but within a family of frameworks, each internally coherent but mutually revisable — locally determinate, globally revisable.

Bartosz Łukasiewicz offered the sharpest critical reading: "The theory of 'art as contextual art' turns out to be yet another archive, yet another collection of truths and norms. The worst thing we could do, however, is to treat it as truth." This is, arguably, the most contextual reading of contextual art — treating the theory itself as subject to its own principle of the "outdating of meanings."

In the 2004 Atlas Sztuki interview, Świdziński reflected on what he had hoped to achieve:

"I read everything they [Art and Language] wrote. And they wrote very chaotically. And I came to the conclusion that it was time to take half a step further. Their art locked itself in a situation where if someone says something is art, then it is art. That's a tautology."Świdziński, Atlas Sztuki, 2004

14. Phase 5: The Expansion — Toronto, Paris, Warsaw (1976–1979)

The eleven months from November 1976 to October 1977 saw contextual art's most intensive period of international activity: three major conferences on three continents.

Toronto, 10–12 November 1976

The CEAC conference, organized by Amerigo Marras, brought Świdziński face to face with Joseph Kosuth. Anna Kutera recalled: "The first words of Kosuth were unambiguous: we will not allow the primacy in the postmodern world to be snatched from us."

Guzek provided the deepest analysis at the TR Warszawa meeting: both men had published manifestos within a year of each other — Kosuth's "Artist as Anthropologist" (The Fox, 1975) and Świdziński's "Art as Contextual Art" (Lund, 1976). "They meet at this moment and see an absolute kinship of thinking," Guzek observed. "But Kosuth didn't think to make context the main category of his art. He reached for anthropologization. And Świdziński didn't think of anthropologization."

Świdziński himself recalled the exchange with characteristic directness:

"I remember how once Kosuth said to me: 'Listen Jan, I had similar ideas later like you' (he said it in the article 'Artist as Anthropologist') — to which I could only reply: 'Too bad you didn't announce them earlier.'"Świdziński, Sztuka i Filozofia, 2006

Paris, 10–13 May 1977

At the "Art et Transformation Sociale" conference, organized by the Collectif Art Sociologique, Świdziński delivered the sentence that may be the single most concise formulation of his project:

"The sense of my work as an artist does not lie in making reality a part of art — as had been proposed in 20th century art from Duchamp, through Happening, Land Art, Body Art — but of making art a part of reality."Świdziński, Paris, May 1977

Warsaw, 1977

At Galeria Remont, the conference "Art as an Action in the Context of Reality" attracted participants from thirty-five countries. Henryk Gajewski edited the bilingual publication — 92 pages, now archived as part of the Art Prints of the Exchange Gallery at the Museum of Modern Art in Warsaw.

Art, Society and Self-consciousness (Calgary, 1979)

The book described by the Ujazdowski Castle Centre as "the most important presentation of the concept of art as contextual art" introduced intentional functors — replacing "this IS art" with "I KNOW/BELIEVE/SUPPOSE this is art" — and the concept of pragmatic restriction. The Polish translation (2009, by Łukasz Guzek) made the text accessible to a new generation of Polish readers.

15. Phase 6: The Crisis — Mielnik, Martial Law, Anomie (1981–1988)

The year 1981 marks the deepest inflection point in contextual art's evolution. The Mielnik project — slogans painted in village streets near the Soviet border during the Solidarity period — was contextual art's most directly political intervention:

"DON'T SUPPOSE ART CAN CHANGE YOUR LIFE EVEN FOR A MOMENT. ART IS AN ILLUSION. AVOID ANY KIND OF ILLUSION. YOU HAVE TO UNDERSTAND YOUR OWN SITUATION. YOU HAVE TO SOLVE YOUR PROBLEMS BY YOURSELF."

Then came martial law. Świdziński's account from the 2006 interview deserves quoting at length:

"From the first day I had an exhibition planned in Kraków titled 'Freedom and Limitation.' I called by phone, but it was out of service, so I got on the train. And there they say martial law. I started laughing. I arrived in Kraków. There was no exhibition. But an interesting thing — they were tearing Solidarity posters off the walls. Everything was being torn down and the only poster hanging was my poster: Jan Świdziński 'Freedom and Limitation'… Finally a friend says to me: 'The censor wants to talk to you.' He gave me the phone number, I called and said: 'Please, sir, I'm happy to fix it, only I don't know what you object to: freedom, or limitation?' The censor fell silent."Świdziński, Sztuka i Filozofia, 2006

This episode — where the title's meaning was determined entirely by its political context, and the censor could not decide which word to suppress — is Thesis 5 enacted as political theater.

The Four Models of Logic

The theoretical response came in Freedom and Limitation — The Anatomy of Post-modernism (Calgary, 1987), which introduced the concept of anomie (from Durkheim) and the four models of logic. The scope expanded from art theory to civilizational diagnosis.

As the notebook sources reveal, the four models regulate different modes of social existence:

  1. Logic of Norms — Full synchronization; essence precedes existence; absolute prohibitions and responsibilities. The pre-modern world of the clock. In art: fixed genres, eternal canons, Malraux's Musée Imaginaire.
  2. Logic of Freedom — Pluralism; "permission" and choice; the split between morality (individual conscience) and law (social consensus). Liberal modernity. In art: the autonomy of the artwork, "art for art's sake," the relativistic model from "Model kina."
  3. Logic of Epistemic Reflection — Distance from moral categories; medicine and psychology replace ethics as reference frameworks. The therapeutic society. In art: the reflexive, self-analyzing artwork that investigates its own conditions of possibility.
  4. Logic of the Game — Truth loses its utility, replaced by profit and success. Situations change minute by minute. No time to learn rules. Aggression is justified. Late capitalism. In art: the market determines value; the artwork as commodity.

These were not presented as a historical succession but as coexisting layers. Anomie — the de-synchronization of meaning structures — arises when the transitions between logics become too rapid for social norms to absorb. Expressions lose their stable referents and become "counting tokens, impossible to connote in infinitely changing, heterogeneous contexts."

The four logics drew on diverse sources: David Hilbert (formal systems), Leon Festinger (cognitive dissonance), Anatol Rapoport (game theory), and Johan Huizinga (Homo Ludens). The ambition was encyclopedic: not merely to describe art's situation but to provide a comprehensive model of how civilizational change produces the conditions within which art must operate.

Świdziński's 2006 interview confirms the connection between anomie and the earlier theory:

"Today this actuality appears in the question of process. And I wrote some twenty years ago that we live in an epoch of increasingly rapid changes that lead to entropy (anomie). Well, what is chaos? — it is some state of our mind toward the things that are occurring around us. And now, because they are constantly changing, I cannot have a fixed point of attachment to the changing situation."Świdziński, Sztuka i Filozofia, 2006

16. Phase 7: The Acceptance — "Jest jak jest" (1995–2014)

The late texts — particularly "Anomia" (Stara Galeria, Lublin, May 1998) — represent Świdziński's final theoretical position: acceptance.

"Where does the title anomia come from? It's not a very popular word. Durkheim introduced it to sociology to denote a state of society when generally accepted norms, values, concepts — the entire commonly understood image of reality — ceases to function. Over twenty years ago, proposing contextual theses for contemporary art, I noticed — because it was staring you in the face — that we were beginning to lack shared norms that would allow for an objective evaluation of art."Świdziński, "Anomia," 1998

And then the tourist metaphor:

"We are somewhat like tourists who stop their car at a roadside pub to rest for a moment, eat a hamburger, drink a beer, and chat with similar tourists resting for a moment like them. Half an hour of rest and on we go."Świdziński, "Anomia," 1998

The "Anomia" text is remarkable for its tone — a departure from the combative precision of the 1976 manifesto toward something more meditative, almost elegiac. Świdziński traces the connection between his early theory and the present:

"Over twenty years ago, proposing contextual theses for contemporary art, I noticed — because it was staring you in the face — that we were beginning to lack shared norms that would allow for an objective evaluation of art. And what's more, we were beginning to lack criteria enabling a clear distinction between what belongs to art and what belongs to another, non-artistic domain. This is the result of changes brought about by ongoing social processes; of this emerging anomie."Świdziński, "Anomia," 1998

Then the tourist metaphor — which would later develop into the "neo-pragmatic tourist" of L'Art et son Contexte (2005):

"It's not about the 'decline of morality' over which traditional moralists wring their hands, seeing in it the cause of the present anomie. We simply stopped living in separation, we watch dozens of television channels, we travel, we meet people of other cultures."Świdziński, "Anomia," 1998

The text ends with a meditation on the loss of Santa Conversatione — the motif in old painting where figures lean toward each other in mutual understanding:

"In old painting we encounter scenes that show figures leaning toward each other. In this symbolic gesture of leaning toward one another, mutual understanding is expressed: Santa Conversatione. In the photographs I show here, people lean toward each other too: but this is not Santa Conversatione, this is simulacrum, the appearance, that anomie of which I speak. Santa Conversatione was once — and now? Now it is what it is. It's okay."Świdziński, "Anomia," 1998

"Jest jak jest. W porządku." — "It is what it is. It's okay." This phrase would become the title of the 2009 retrospective. It is contextual art's final statement: not a theory, not a manifesto, not a program, but an acceptance — pragmatic, unsentimental, precise — of the condition that the theory had spent thirty years describing.

In the last recorded assessment of his work, the 2006 interview, Świdziński offered what may be his most revealing self-characterization:

"The only thing I can say about art is that it is not the kind of art it once was. There are cultural activities as they are. This is pragmatism. A view without preconceptions that allows one to notice that changeability is interesting."Świdziński, Sztuka i Filozofia, 2006

The hidden autobiography runs parallel: from double agent (inside/outside) through coerced collaborator (meaning imposed by power) through failed painter (artistic emptiness) through militant theorist (building coalitions) to philosophical tourist (moving lightly through contexts without mastering them).

What is beyond dispute is the existential dimension. The theory and the life both address the same problem: how to maintain coherence — intellectual, moral, existential — in a world where meaning is radically unstable. The wartime generation in Poland, regardless of individual circumstances, lived through conditions where a poster titled "Freedom and Limitation" could become a political statement simply by surviving when Solidarity posters are torn down, where the same word — "freedom" — meant different things to the underground, the occupier, and the postwar state. Świdziński's "it is what it is" is not resignation but the deepest form of attention to the actual conditions of existence — conditions he knew not from theory but from decades of living through systems that competed to impose meaning on reality.

PART III: THE PRACTICE
Performance as Contextual Medium

18. Why Performance? The Empty Sign and the Body

Performance art was not merely one medium among others for Świdziński — it was the privileged medium of contextual art, the practice that most fully realized the theoretical principles of the manifesto. In an interview with Łukasz Guzek, he stated the logic plainly:

"Performance is the most important practice" because it "refers to the origins of art, when what we consider as art, has not yet existed."Jan Świdziński, interview with Guzek

The reasoning was rigorous. If meaning is determined by the pragmatic context — the specific conjunction of time, place, situation, and persons — then the art form most thoroughly embedded in those coordinates would be the one that most fully realizes the contextual principle. Performance, by its nature, is unrepeatable: each occurs at a unique time, in a unique place, for a unique audience. It cannot be stored, transported, or reproduced without fundamental transformation. It is, in the fullest sense, an "empty sign."

Świdziński drew a fundamental distinction between two types of human activity: producing something versus expressing and communicating through gesture, words, and language. Performance embodies the latter. As he told Guzek: "The role of the spectator was to recognize what the artist wanted to say… Today he has an active one." The spectator's presence, attention, and interpretive activity become constitutive elements of the work itself.

But there was also a biographical dimension. The man who had been a ballet student — training under Leon Wójcikowski, "the successor to Nijinsky" — understood the body as an expressive medium from his youth. The wartime dancer became the postwar painter became the late-career performer: a trajectory that moved from trained physical expression through visual representation to the direct deployment of bodily presence in specific situations.

Bartosz Łukasiewicz, in the Sztuka i Dokumentacja article, described the philosophy behind Świdziński's performance practice:

"Art is not like life — it is its part. It is not its image, nor its model, nor even its equivalent. Such art is not a description. It happens as it is, acts on the basis of the situation of its participants, freely, not at all, in the ongoing process of change… We are never performers — what we do, we submit to some force and transmit it further."Łukasiewicz, "Model logiki a logika modelu," SiD, 2010

19. Early Actions: Van Gogh, Chairs, and the Kurpie Ethnography (1974–1978)

Świdziński began performing in 1974, the same year he formulated his initial contextual art theses. His first documented performance (Kraków, 1976) involved surrounding himself with chairs and then scattering them — a simple action with political overtones: the chairs as constraints, their scattering as liberation.

The performance with the Van Gogh photograph, presented at the Lund exhibition in February 1976, was the key early work. By incorporating a reproduction of a canonical artwork into a live performance, Świdziński staged the collision between two modes of meaning: the art-historical meaning accumulated over a century and the immediate, situational meaning generated by the live encounter. As sources describe it, the action demonstrated "the slippage between the history of art and the present reality of the artist's action."

Anna Kutera, at the TR Warszawa meeting, recalled the sparseness of Świdziński's artistic contribution to the Lund exhibition: "Świdziński showed only one work at the large Lund exhibition — his work with the rotation of a photograph showing Van Gogh's back, and the '12 points.' Each of us others tried to show practice as much as possible." This observation — that the founder of contextual art contributed primarily theory rather than objects — would become a recurring theme in the critical reception.

In 1977–78, Świdziński undertook local actions in the Kurpie region and in Mielnik (the latter fully realized in 1981). These were ethnographic interventions — contextual art applied to rural communities. As the notebook sources describe: "Projects of contextual art inspired by the ethnography of local cultures." These actions represented the practical consequence of the theoretical commitment to localism: the artist as "ethnologist-therapist" (Piotrowski's later term) entering a specific community and using art to make that community conscious of its own context.

"Stale mówimy o człowieku" (We Constantly Speak About the Human Being, 1978) consisted of a single "found" photograph combined with a sentence. The minimalism of means placed maximum emphasis on the contextual coordinates of reception. The work was exhibited at Mała Galeria on 26 June 1979.

20. "Freedom and Limitation": Slogans, Censors, and the Solidarity Context (1981)

The Mielnik project of 1981 has already been discussed in theoretical terms (Chapter 15). Here we attend to its practical dimensions.

The village of Mielnik, located near the Soviet border, was chosen deliberately. Świdziński led artists out of the gallery and into the streets, where they painted slogans — de-mythologizing statements that refused to attribute transformative power to art. The timing was critical: the "Carnival of Solidarity," the period of extraordinary political openness between the Gdańsk Agreements of August 1980 and martial law of December 1981.

The slogans functioned as "empty signs" — their meaning inseparable from the political situation in which they were encountered. "Don't suppose art can change your life even for a moment" was, in the context of a society engaged in collective self-liberation, both a philosophical position and a provocation. It refused the instrumentalization of art for political purposes — whether by the Communist state or by the opposition.

The project was documented in a publication, Wolność i ograniczenie Mielnik 1981, published by Mała Galeria in 1984. The martial law censor episode — where the censor could not determine which word in "Freedom and Limitation" to suppress — remains the most vivid demonstration of the contextual thesis in action.

21. Blurring, Two Monitors, and the Anomic Works (1983–1990)

The 1980s saw Świdziński develop a distinctive performance language characterized by sparse gestures, limited means, and a darkening theoretical horizon.

"Blurring (Thinking of Heidegger)" (1985) explored the dissolution of clear boundaries — art/non-art, meaning/non-meaning, presence/absence. The video documentation is held in the Filmoteka of the Museum of Modern Art in Warsaw. "Two Monitors" (1985) was a video installation exploring the "morphology of media" through the juxtaposition of two screens — the same information presented through different channels producing different meanings.

"Jestem z powrotem" (I'm Back) (Mała Galeria, 4 April 1989) — the performance of return from emigration, weeks before the Round Table Agreements. "Ça marche, ça marche pas" (1990) — the first post-Communist work, critiquing "the subordination of art to market dictates" in the new capitalist reality. "Where We Are Going Baby?" (Nagano, Japan, 1993) — analyzing locality within globalization.

Each of these works enacted a different aspect of the theoretical framework: Blurring tested the dissolution of categories (Thesis 7); Two Monitors tested the contextual determination of meaning through media; I'm Back tested the identity function under contextual change; Ça marche tested the shift from ideological to market-driven meaning-production.

22. "Jan in Wonderland" and the Performative Quotation (1991–2010)

The long-running series "Jan in Wonderland" (1991–2009) was Świdziński's most sustained performance project. The title's allusion to Lewis Carroll suggests a world where ordinary logic is suspended and meaning is generated through arbitrary, context-dependent rules.

The 2010 iteration was particularly significant: a "performed quotation" in which Świdziński sat reading Carroll's Alice in Wonderland from a laptop, simultaneously broadcast online. The live audience saw his full physical presence; the digital audience saw only a close-up of his face. This dual delivery tested the theory that context is never final — the same "object" (the reading) acquired radically different meanings depending on the medium of reception.

Katarzyna Podpora analyzed this last major project in detail, exploring how the "performative quotation" treated text fragments as "ready-made objects" whose meaning shifted with each contextual instantiation.

23. Photography and Text as Contextual Media

Alongside performance, Świdziński made extensive use of photography and text. His "actions with photography and text" — the subtitle of the 2010 CSW Zamek Ujazdowski retrospective — treated photographs and sentences not as autonomous objects but as elements within contextual configurations.

A found photograph, a handwritten sentence, a Van Gogh reproduction — these were the raw materials. The works were deliberately simple in material constitution, because complexity of material would have distracted from the complexity of contextual signification. As Grzegorz Borkowski wrote in his critical analyses, the photographs operated as "documents of encounters" rather than aesthetic compositions — their value lay not in their formal qualities but in the contextual relations they embodied.

The 2009 retrospective "Jest jak jest" at ODA Gallery, curated by Guzek, featured the contextual art formula displayed above the gallery entrance — a gesture that transformed the gallery space itself into a contextual sign: visitors entered not a neutral exhibition space but a theoretically framed context.

Zbigniew Warpechowski, in the dialogue recorded by Przyłuski, captured the essence of Świdziński's approach to performance:

Świdziński: "That is probably the essence of performance, that with you one never knows what will happen." Warpechowski (laughing): "Yes!" Świdziński: "That is the most interesting thing about it."Dialogue in Przyłuski, Sztuka akcji, 2007

PART IV: THE RECEPTION
Three Schools of Interpretation

24. Kazimierz Piotrowski: Nonmonotonic Aesthetics and the Ethnologist-Therapist

Kazimierz Piotrowski is the most extensive scholarly interpreter of Świdziński's work. His engagement spans three decades — from the 1996 EXIT article to the 840-page monograph Świdziński i współcześni (2015) — and constitutes an independent contribution to aesthetic theory that transcends mere interpretation.

The Logical Foundation

Piotrowski's central innovation is the application of nonmonotonic logic to aesthetics. In classical (monotonic) logic, adding new premises never invalidates existing conclusions. In nonmonotonic logic (developed from the late 1970s by Reiter, McDermott, McCarthy), conclusions are defeasible — revisable when new information arrives.

Piotrowski argues that the "outdating of meanings" in Thesis 5 has precisely this structure: a meaning valid in one context becomes invalid when contextual coordinates change. The contextual truth-function is a default rule: valid absent countervailing information, overridable when the situation shifts.

He illustrates with the example of a Kazakh peasant who, confronted with a formal syllogism about bears in the North Pole, refuses to draw the "logical" conclusion because it contradicts his experiential knowledge. The peasant's reasoning is nonmonotonic: he privileges context-specific knowledge over abstract deduction. This, Piotrowski argues, is precisely the kind of reasoning contextual art demands — and that monotonic (classical) aesthetics cannot accommodate.

Nonmonotonic Logic: A Technical Excursus

For readers unfamiliar with the logical background: in classical (monotonic) propositional logic, the principle of monotonicity guarantees that if a conclusion C follows from a set of premises P, then C also follows from any superset of P. Adding new information never invalidates previously valid conclusions. This property makes classical logic well-suited to mathematics but poorly suited to everyday reasoning, where new information routinely overturns established conclusions. (You believe the bird can fly because you know it is a bird; you revise this belief when you learn it is a penguin.)

Nonmonotonic logics — developed from the late 1970s by Raymond Reiter, Drew McDermott, and John McCarthy — formalize this kind of defeasible reasoning. Conclusions may be tentatively derived from available premises but can be retracted when new premises are introduced. The conclusion is not invalid — it was correct given the available information — but it is defeasible: subject to revision in light of new data.

Piotrowski's crucial insight is that the "outdating of meanings" in Thesis 5 has precisely this logical structure. A meaning established in one context is valid in that context but becomes invalid — is "outdated" — when the context changes. The contextual truth-function is, in formal terms, a default rule: a rule that holds in the absence of countervailing information but that can be overridden when contextual coordinates change.

This formal analysis has several important consequences. First, it explains how contextual art can be simultaneously anti-relativist and radically context-dependent: the truth of an aesthetic judgment is locally determined but globally revisable. Second, it provides a precise account of what it means for a meaning to "become outdated": not that the meaning was wrong but that the premises on which it was based no longer obtain. Third, it connects contextual art to contemporary developments in artificial intelligence (where nonmonotonic reasoning models belief revision) and epistemology (where contextualism has become a major position in the theory of knowledge).

"Aesthetic thinking develops and today follows the newest logics. Therefore I too could allow myself such defeasible reasoning, listing a series of premises…"Kazimierz Piotrowski
"The point is to expand the concept of normalcy also in thinking about art."Kazimierz Piotrowski

Acutum Ingenium and Asteiologia

Drawing on Baumgarten's aesthetics and the Baroque tradition of Baltasar Gracián, Piotrowski rehabilitates wit (ingenium) as a serious cognitive tool. Acutum ingenium (sharp wit) combines ingenium (perceiving similarities among heterogeneous things) with acumen (detecting differences). Together they form perspicacia — penetration that is both sensory and intellectual.

Asteiologia — the "art, science, and paideia of wit" — is Piotrowski's proposed aesthetic discipline: a mode of thinking that uses "discordant concord" (Gracián's agudeza) to expose what monological thinking conceals. He proposes asteism — wit deprived of coarseness — as a pharmakon (both medicine and poison) against the "anesthesia of contemporary post-art."

In this framework, Świdziński's contextual art is itself an exercise in acutum ingenium: the sharp insight that remodels the artwork from Kosuth's tautology into a sentence containing an intentional functor.

Defocalization

Piotrowski's most politically charged application of nonmonotonic aesthetics is defocalization (defoksalizacja) — the dismantling of the Foksal Gallery's monopoly on the narrative of Polish contemporary art. The term refers to a strategic deployment of defeasible reasoning: introducing new premises (Świdziński, Partum, Kulik as "masters") that overturn the established Foksal canon.

The Foksal debate has deep roots. In 1975, Foksal's director Wiesław Borowski published "Pseudoawangarda" in the weekly Kultura, condemning practices outside the Foksal circle as "pretentious" and "hidden in pseudo-scientific jargon." Piotrowski interprets this not as art criticism but as a rhetorical move in a power game: Foksal's circle "reported on certain art to the PRL authorities" through the socialist press.

Together with Zbigniew Libera, Piotrowski carried out an act of "official defocalization" through Libera's "Mistrzowie" (Masters) project — introducing into circulation artists whom Foksal had marginalized. Borowski's circle had mocked Świdziński's project as "provincial" — precisely the quality contextual theory valorized.

The Ethnologist-Therapist and Irrelevance

Piotrowski proposes the ethnologist-therapist as a model for the contextual artist: someone who studies cultural contexts (like an ethnologist) while intervening therapeutically to expose outdated meanings. The artist visits local contexts, helps "indigenous" inhabitants recognize their own distinctiveness, and moves on — a characterization that maps onto Świdziński's own "neo-pragmatic tourist."

Most provocatively, Piotrowski valorizes irrelevance as an aesthetic category. The figure of "Hadassa" — a viewer completely indifferent to canonical artworks — represents not failure but a legitimate contextual response. Monotonic aesthetics treats Hadassa's indifference as an error; nonmonotonic aesthetics treats it as evidence that meaning does not transfer across contexts.

"The point is to expand the concept of normalcy also in thinking about art."Kazimierz Piotrowski

25. Łukasz Ronduda: Reverse Pioneering and the Pragmatic Turn

Łukasz Ronduda's contribution operates at the level of art historiography rather than formal logic. His landmark essay "Flexibility Makes Our Existence Possible" (ARTMargins, 2008) and his book Polish Art of the 70s (2009, with Piotr Uklański) repositioned contextual art within a comprehensive reappraisal of the Polish neo-avant-garde.

The Post-Essentialist to Pragmatist Spectrum

Ronduda organizes the 1970s along a spectrum from post-essentialism (isolating art's conceptual essence from social influence) to pragmatism (activist involvement in reality). Świdziński occupies the pivotal position: his theory marks the decisive turn from analyzing art's structure to immersing art in social practice.

Reverse Pioneering

Ronduda's most original concept: the "masters" of the 1970s are being creatively "created" by artists of the present to fill voids in canonical art history. Contemporary success generates demand for historical precedents — earlier artists retroactively "discovered" and canonized. The temporal direction of influence reverses: contemporary artists create their predecessors.

This concept challenges linear art history — and demonstrates, at the historiographic level, the same contextual principle Świdziński articulated at the aesthetic level: meaning is determined by contexts of reception, not origins.

Świdziński as "Post-Conceptual, Post-Modern Oskar Hansen"

Ronduda's comparison links contextual art to Hansen's "Open Form" — the architectural theory that rejected modernist spatial purity in favor of flexibility, participation, and responsiveness. Both theories share an anti-authoritarian core: Hansen rejected the architect's control over spatial use, as Świdziński rejected the artist's control over meaning.

The Artist Cast

Ronduda positions Świdziński alongside: KwieKulik (Soc Art, "performance for the camera"), Akademia Ruchu (urban actions, "common gestures"), Workshop of the Film Form (structural film, "morphology of media"), Ewa Partum (feminist conceptualism), and Anastazy Wiśniewski (institutional parody). Each represents a different facet of the pragmatic turn.

The Piotrowski–Ronduda Tension

Piotrowski has criticized Ronduda's methodology, claiming he "darkened the picture of Polish art of the 70s" by "mixing different orders of narration." The tension is genuine: Ronduda seeks accessible narrative (aided by Uklański's striking visual design), Piotrowski demands philosophical rigor. The debate itself demonstrates the contextual thesis: two interpreters, different institutional contexts, different meanings from the same material.

26. Łukasz Guzek: Horizontal Art History and the Documentation Paradox

Guzek represents a third approach — grounded in documentation theory and what he calls "horizontal art history" (influenced by Piotr Piotrowski's methodology): viewing art from the "periphery" as equal to that of major centers.

The Documentation Paradox

Contextual art raises a fundamental question: how do you document an art form whose essential claim is that meaning is inseparable from the specific context of enactment? Any documentation — photograph, video, description — captures something other than the work. The document is a new sign in a new context.

Guzek navigates this through what he calls "reconstruction" — treating the study of performance as itself a performative engagement with historical material. His book Rekonstrukcja sztuki akcji w Polsce (The Reconstruction of Action Art in Poland) does not merely describe performances but reconstructs them as objects of contemporary scholarly attention.

Conferences as Artworks

Guzek's most provocative claim: symposia, conferences, and gallery-founding should be studied as works of art themselves. The 1977 Warsaw conference with 35 countries was not merely a venue for theory — it was a contextual artwork. The INTERAKCJE festival was Świdziński's most sustained performance.

Guzek's key contribution at the TR Warszawa meeting was his analysis of the Kosuth-Świdziński convergence — the recognition that both men had arrived at the same "absolutely breakthrough moment" in the modernism-to-postmodernism transition, but from different routes: Kosuth through "anthropologization," Świdziński through "contextualization."

He confirmed Świdziński's role as "the pen" of the Polish neo-avant-garde: "It is the Workshop of the Film Form that took Janek, and he writes for them this important text called 'Model kina.'"

27. Bartosz Łukasiewicz: "A Few Dogmas of Contextualism"

Łukasiewicz, the youngest of the major interpreters, brought critical rigor to what had sometimes been uncritical appreciation. His MA thesis Kontekst i znak w teorii sztuki Jana Świdzińskiego (Context and Sign in Świdziński's Art Theory, Warsaw University, 2006) and his subsequent articles — "Model logiki a logika modelu" (SiD, 2010) and "Kilka dogmatów kontekstualizmu" (Obieg, 2010) — tested contextual art theory against its own standards.

Drawing on Eco's theory of models and Bridgman's operationalism, Łukasiewicz argued that Świdziński's logical models are operational rather than representational: "They are not patterns or copies of reality. They are one of the ways in which reality can be grasped." This is a more modest claim than Świdziński sometimes seemed to make.

His critical challenge was sharp: "If time and place have stopped having any material reference, if they have become virtual — then does building such a system of reference make any sense?" And his conclusion was deliberately unsettling:

"The theory of 'art as contextual art' turns out to be yet another archive, yet another collection of truths and norms. The worst thing we could do, however, is to treat it as truth. It will never be fully true, nor will it ever be fully rejected, just as truths claiming to be right will never be fully verified. Perhaps it is simply a matter of not accepting anything on faith or out of habit, but of constantly rejecting, verifying, and redefining."Bartosz Łukasiewicz, SiD, 2010

This is, arguably, the most contextual reading of contextual art: treating the theory itself as subject to its own principle of the "outdating of meanings."

28. Zbigniew Libera: "The Contextual Gospel According to Jan"

Zbigniew Libera's 2020 Przekrój article, discussed extensively in Part I, represents a fundamentally different kind of reception: not scholarly interpretation but biographical investigation by a practicing artist. Libera — one of Poland's most important contemporary artists, creator of "Lego: Concentration Camp" and other iconic critical works — had included Świdziński in his "Mistrzowie" (Masters) series in 2004, "but I must admit that at that time, while the elderly Świdziński was still alive, I did not yet know everything that today so demands illumination."

Libera's article is significant as the first attempt to examine Świdziński's biography through archival research, but its claims — particularly regarding Kripo and UB — have not been independently verified by IPN historians or any other neutral expert. The article appeared six years after Świdziński's death, when he could no longer respond or provide context. SB archival materials require specialist interpretation; their raw content can mislead non-expert readers. Until independent verification is conducted, these claims should be treated as one artist's interpretation of fragmentary evidence, not as established facts.

What is valuable in Libera's article — beyond the unverified allegations — is the richly documented family history (the father's Legions service, the noble lineage, the wartime ballet studies), the account of the failed painting career and the Institut Français self-education, and the connection between Sellem and the Situationist International. These elements, which do not depend on IPN materials, enrich our understanding of Świdziński's trajectory.

Libera connects the theory to the life through Piotrowski's formulation: "Perhaps without Świdziński's existential entanglement we would not be witnesses to 'Jan's contextual gospel' resulting from 'the passionate liberation of oneself from the pressure of context in a civilization of rapid changes by means of nonmonotonic logic and aesthetics — against the limitation of context.'" This reading — which treats the theory as emerging from lived experience of contextual instability — is compelling regardless of which specific biographical details are ultimately confirmed.

29. Jan Lubicz Przyłuski: The Filmmaker and the Contextual Legacy

Jan Lubicz Przyłuski — visual artist, anthropologist, filmmaker — occupies a unique position: simultaneously a scholar, a creative collaborator, and a personal friend of Świdziński. His first documentary film, Differences (2006, 50 minutes), is dedicated to Świdziński and opens with the artist's statements before transitioning into "pure performance actions." He conducted a twelve-hour "interview-river" with Świdziński — a primary source resource that remains only partially exploited.

Przyłuski co-organized the "Różnice/Differences" festival (from 2006), which later moved to CSW Zamek Ujazdowski. In January 2010, he lectured on contextual art and film at the Bezalel Academy of Fine Arts in Jerusalem — carrying contextual theory to new geographical and institutional contexts.

His text on contextual art provides one of the most eloquent popular introductions:

"The language of art and the language of logic could not go hand in hand until the time of the theory of plurality, until the announcement of pluralistic and many-valued logic. From this, Świdziński drew consequences that others feared."Jan Lubicz Przyłuski, "Jesteśmy zaplątani," 2010

His book Sztuka akcji. Dziesięć zdarzeń w Polsce (Action Art: Ten Events in Poland, 2007) situates Świdziński within a broader narrative of Polish performance art, including the recorded Świdziński-Warpechowski dialogue on improvisation and unpredictability.

30. The International Network: From CEAC to Le Lieu

Contextual art's international network was not merely a distribution system; it was a geopolitical statement. The key nodes:

CEAC, Toronto (1976)

The Centre for Experimental Art and Communication, co-founded by Amerigo Marras and Suber Donald Corley at 15 Duncan Street. CEAC published the journal STRIKE and hosted the decisive 1976 conference. Though short-lived (dissolved by early 1980s), its role as catalyst for Świdziński's international breakthrough was decisive.

Le Lieu, Quebec City (from 1982)

Richard Martel's centre en art actuel and Éditions Intervention became contextual art's most enduring institutional partner. Three books published. Martel contributed to the 2015 In Context volume. Le Lieu functioned "in accordance with contextual art postulates" — a rare case of an institution explicitly modeling itself on an artist's theory.

Galerie S:t Petri, Lund

Jean Sellem — connected, as Libera revealed, to the Situationist International — named the movement and published the manifesto. The first press review of contextual art appeared in Skånska Dagbladet (29 February 1976) by Torsten Weimarck.

Art Sociologique, Paris

Hervé Fischer, Fred Forest, Jean-Paul Thénot — allied with Świdziński in the "Third Front" but criticized by him for "lack of learning." The alliance formalized at the May 1977 Paris conference but ultimately "fell prey to its own assumptions of relativism."

Het Apollohuis, Eindhoven

Paul Panhuysen published Quotations on Contextual Art (1988) — the polyphonic text assembling voices from the Toronto debate.

Canadian Connections: Calgary, Vancouver, NSCAD

Świdziński's Canadian connections extended well beyond CEAC. He lived in Canada for extended periods during the 1980s, holding an open Canadian visa and lecturing at the University of Calgary and the University of British Columbia in Vancouver. Calgary was particularly important as a publishing base: both Art, Society and Self-consciousness (1979) and Freedom and Limitation (1987, published by Brian Dyson's Syntax Publishing) appeared there. Calgary's own "peripheral" position within North American art resonated with Świdziński's theoretical commitment to non-metropolitan production.

He visited the United States twelve times during this period, staying months each time. In New York, he befriended Joseph Kosuth, continuing the dialogue begun in Toronto. The friendship personified the center-periphery dialogue: a Polish contextualist and an American conceptualist, their exchange persisting across Cold War divisions.

The transition from CEAC (anglophone Toronto) to Le Lieu (francophone Quebec) as Świdziński's primary Canadian partner opened new publishing opportunities and connected contextual art to Quebec's distinct cultural politics — its own experience of linguistic marginality within Canada.

Bruce Barber (NSCAD, Halifax) — interdisciplinary artist, cultural historian, and professor — contributed an essay on Polish-Canadian artistic relations to the 2015 In Context volume, documenting a pattern of exchange that reflects structural similarities: both Canadian and Polish artists experienced their art worlds as peripheral and grappled with creating significant work from positions of relative marginality.

INTERAKCJE: The Festival as Contextual Art

The International Festival of the Art of Action INTERAKCJE, organized in Piotrków Trybunalski from 1999, was the most sustained institutional embodiment of contextual art principles — and, as Jan Przyłuski argued at the TR Warszawa meeting, a refutation of the criticism that contextual art never became a "movement":

"He created the world's only fifteen-year-running performance art festival, at world level, in Piotrków Trybunalski. Isn't that a contradiction — wouldn't that be a central action, contradictory to the idea of local action that Świdziński promoted?"Jan Lubicz Przyłuski, TR Warszawa, 2014

The choice of Piotrków Trybunalski — a medium-sized industrial city far from Warsaw, Kraków, or Wrocław — was itself a contextual statement: meaningful artistic exchange could occur outside established infrastructure. The city's particular history — medieval royal town, industrial center, site of wartime deportation — provided layers of contextual meaning that inflected every performance within its borders.

Świdziński served as President and Curator, together with Ryszard Piegza as organizational partner. In 2008, both received the Bronze Medal for Services to Piotrków Trybunalski. The festival hosted artists from dozens of countries, creating one of the most important international platforms for performance art in Central and Eastern Europe. The 2006–2007 editions (organized with Jan Przyłuski as "Różnice/Differences") brought participants from Israel, Italy, France, Canada, Netherlands, Germany, USA, Singapore, and Poland — including Bruce Barber, Paul Panhuysen, Artur Tajber, Waldemar Tatarczuk, Bartosz Łukasiewicz, Zbigniew Warpechowski, and Ewa Zarzycka.

Guzek argued that the festival should be studied as a work of art itself. Under Świdziński's curatorial direction, it functioned not merely as a venue for presenting performance but as a large-scale contextual artwork — a temporary community engaged in collective meaning-production within specific coordinates. International artists performing at INTERAKCJE were contextual tourists: visiting a specific place for a limited time, establishing intense but temporary contacts, producing meanings inseparable from the conditions of their production.

Świdziński's death in February 2014 raised the question of whether a festival so closely identified with a single figure could survive its founder. Its continuation tests contextual art's central thesis: meaning is not carried by individuals but generated by pragmatic context. The festival without Świdziński is not the same festival — the pragmatic coordinates have changed — but it continues to instantiate the principle that the theory outlives the theorist.

The Gallery Network in Poland

The distribution of contextual art texts relied on a remarkable network of artist-run galleries — each an autonomous node in a decentralized system that prefigured the networking logics of the digital age:

  • Galeria Remont (Warsaw) — directed by Henryk Gajewski; published the 92-page bilingual manifesto (March 1977, Art Text imprint, 14 × 19.5 cm); organized the 1977 international conference and Gajewski's "I AM" event (1978)
  • Mała Galeria (Warsaw) — co-founded by Świdziński and Dłubak; one of the first Polish galleries to exhibit photography as autonomous art; part of the Artist Run Initiatives (ARI) movement; hosted five Świdziński solo shows (1979–1994) and Ewa Partum's radical "Samoidentyfikacja" (1980)

Contextual Art and Other Anti-Institutional Movements

Situationism shared contextual art's commitment to dissolving the boundary between art and life. Debord's concept of the spectacle resonates with Świdziński's "apparent reality" (Thesis 4). But Situationism prescribed the deliberate construction of "situations," while contextual art insisted that even the artist's own interventions are subject to contextual determination. The Sellem-Debord connection (noted by Libera) suggests a closer genealogy that remains unexplored.

Fluxus anticipated contextual art's fusion of art and life, particularly through the "event score." But Fluxus remained committed to anti-art that defined itself in opposition to institutions, while contextual art theorized the mechanism by which definitions are produced and "outdated."

Institutional critique (Broodthaers, Haacke, Buren, Fraser) shares contextual art's attention to institutional frameworks. But institutional critique typically operates within institutions, while contextual art operates across all social contexts — the museum is one pragmatic context among many.

Social practice art (Hirschhorn, Bruguera, Lowe) represents the closest contemporary analogue. Like Świdziński, these artists embed practice in specific communities. But where social practice emphasizes the ethical dimension, contextual art emphasizes the epistemological — the two approaches are complementary.

31. The Foksal Debate and the "Pseudoawangarda" Attack

The Foksal Gallery, directed by Wiesław Borowski, represented the establishment against which contextual art positioned itself. Borowski's 1975 article "Pseudoawangarda" in Kultura condemned practices outside the Foksal circle, mocking Świdziński's project as "provincial."

Piotrowski divides the scene into the "powerless avant-garde" adapted to bureaucratic rules (Foksal) and the "revalued pseudo-avant-garde" (contextualists) operating outside its walls. The tension maps onto the broader center-periphery dynamic: Warsaw establishment mimicking New York fashions versus Świdziński's defense of the periphery.

Anna Kutera, at the TR meeting, expressed the frustration of those who expected a movement: "The youth who followed Świdziński expected that a movement would emerge. Something on a large scale, through which we could enter discussions with such great movements as conceptualism or Fluxus." But Przyłuski offered a rebuttal: "Wouldn't that be a central action, contradictory to the idea of local action that Świdziński promoted — and he created the world's only fifteen-year-running performance art festival, at world level, in Piotrków Trybunalski?"

PART V: LEGACY AND CONTEMPORARY RESONANCE

32. Contextual Art and Relational Aesthetics: Bourriaud, Hansen, Ardenne

Nicolas Bourriaud's Relational Aesthetics (1998) proposed that contemporary art's task was producing social relations rather than autonomous objects — echoing Świdziński's 1976 Thesis 9: contextual art is concerned with "the relation itself." Both drew on left-oriented aesthetic theory, post-structuralist semiotics, and pragmatist philosophy. But Świdziński arrived at the relational position two decades earlier and articulated it with greater philosophical rigor — a fact that underscores the belatedness of international engagement with Eastern European artistic production.

Where Bourriaud's relational aesthetics has been criticized for political vagueness — celebrating conviviality without analyzing power structures — Świdziński's contextual art provides a sharper analytical framework: the pragmatic truth-function that specifies which contextual coordinates determine meaning, and the anti-hegemonic commitment that explicitly addresses the politics of cultural legitimation. Contextual art is relational aesthetics with teeth.

Paul Ardenne's L'Art Contextuel (2002) — which, as Guzek confirmed at the TR meeting, "begins with a quotation from Świdziński" — provided a French theoretical framework that popularized the concept internationally. But as Przyłuski noted at the same meeting, "Ardenne, invited by Świdziński, does not respond, does not come to Poland" — a microcosm of the center-periphery dynamic that contextual theory critiques. At the TR meeting, Guzek disputed Przyłuski's characterization: "No, no. Ardenne knows perfectly well… He wrote that book fully aware of what Świdziński had written and he quotes him." The exchange reveals the continuing tension around recognition and attribution.

The Hansen Connection

The comparison with Oskar Hansen's Open Form (proposed by Ronduda) is architecturally precise. Hansen (1922–2005), the visionary architect and pedagogue, developed a theory that rejected modernist spatial purity as incompatible with the real contradictions of social life. Open Form prioritized flexibility, user participation, and responsiveness to changing conditions — an architectural analogue of contextual art's insistence on the primacy of reception over intention.

Both theories shared an anti-authoritarian core: Hansen rejected the architect's claim to determine how space would be used, as Świdziński rejected the artist's claim to determine how signs would be read. Both proposed open, participatory frameworks in which meaning (spatial or aesthetic) was generated by users/viewers in specific contexts. And both emerged from the specific conditions of Polish intellectual life under socialism — where the question of who controls meaning was not abstract but politically urgent.

Artists like KwieKulik developed Hansen's ideas into performative extensions and situational collages, bridging Open Form's architectural concerns with the semiotic preoccupations of contextual art. The lineage from Hansen through Świdziński to contemporary participatory practice constitutes a distinctly Polish contribution to the international discourse on relational, social, and context-responsive art.

Site-Specificity and Institutional Critique

Contextual art is fundamentally connected to site-specific tendencies — it posits that an artistic object assumes meaning only in specific coordinates. But where site-specific art (Smithson, Serra, Buren) focused on the physical and institutional characteristics of the site, contextual art expanded "site" to include the full pragmatic context — social, economic, cultural, political, interpersonal. The site is not a location but a situation.

And where institutional critique (Broodthaers, Haacke, Fraser) operates within institutions to expose hidden power structures, contextual art operates across institutions and non-institutions alike. The museum is one pragmatic context among many; the street, the village, the mail network, and the private apartment are equally legitimate.

33. The Post-Truth Condition and the Outdating of Meanings

Thesis 4 anticipated with remarkable precision the epistemological crisis now called "post-truth": "Because of the diversified range of meanings in the definition systems, there is the possibility of constructing apparent meanings of reality, and in consequence, of producing an apparent reality."

The production of "apparent reality" through sign-system manipulation — disinformation, fake news, post-truth politics — is precisely the pathology contextual art diagnosed. The contextual artist's "steady rejection of canons" (Thesis 6) is prophylaxis against the meaning-ossification that enables ideological manipulation.

But contextual art also explains why post-truth arises: accelerating civilizational change produces "outdating of meanings" that leaves sign systems unable to represent reality. Into this gap rush "apparent meanings" — ideological constructions simulating coherence. The appropriate response is not returning to fixed, universal meaning (impossible and undesirable) but deepening contextual awareness — the skill contextual art was designed to develop.

34. Digital Context: AI, Algorithms, and the Empty Sign

Large language models generate text by predicting contextually appropriate continuations — a computational implementation of the contextual truth-function. Yet AI's "context" is statistical (co-occurrence patterns) rather than pragmatic (time, place, situation, persons). It lacks the embodied, situated, interpersonal dimension that makes human meaning-making irreducible to algorithmic prediction.

The tension between computational and pragmatic context — between statistical prediction and lived meaning — is likely to become a central aesthetic question. Świdziński's framework provides philosophical tools for navigating it.

Digital media also challenge performance art's privileged position in contextual theory. Performance's unreproducibility — its inseparability from specific coordinates — is undermined by digital documentation and distribution. What happens to the "empty sign" when it is filled by the context of a YouTube video rather than a live encounter? The theory did not address this — but provides tools to investigate it.

36. Open Questions for Future Research

  • Gender and contextual art. Ewa Partum, Anna Kutera, Zofia Kulik played crucial roles. How does gender function as a contextual coordinate in Thesis 5's formula?
  • The digital context. How do the twelve theses function when "pragmatic context" is partly constituted by automated systems?
  • Comparative contextualism. Indian rasa theory, Japanese ma, indigenous relational ontologies — what would comparative study reveal?
  • Archival recovery. The Fundacja Arton holds Świdziński's archive. Przyłuski's twelve-hour interview-river is only partially exploited.
  • Non-European reception. Nagano 1993, Argentine mail art connections, Przyłuski's Jerusalem lecture — unexplored.
  • The Libera claims. The allegations regarding Kripo and UB published in Przekrój (2020) have not been independently verified by IPN experts. Independent archival research by qualified historians is needed before these claims can be accepted as biographical fact. Until then, they should be treated with scholarly caution — particularly given that they appeared after Świdziński's death, when he could not respond.
  • The Situationist connection. Sellem's link to Debord's Situationist International is noted by Libera but unexplored. What are the theoretical implications?
  • Contextual pedagogy. Świdziński lectured at universities across four continents. What pedagogical methods did he develop for transmitting a theory that insists on the untransferability of meaning?
  • The INTERAKCJE festival. The only fifteen-year-running international performance art festival in a provincial Polish city — a unique institutional experiment. Its continuation after 2014 tests whether the theory outlives the theorist.

Toward a Theory of Contextual Criticism

If meaning is always context-dependent, what are the conditions of possibility for art criticism? If no aesthetic judgment transcends its context, then the critic's claims are themselves contextually situated and subject to "outdating." The history of contextual art criticism demonstrates this: Piotrowski's nonmonotonic aesthetics, Ronduda's reverse pioneering, Guzek's horizontal art history — each generation produces new meanings from the same material, and these meanings are not cumulative but revisable.

This does not render criticism impossible; it renders criticism contextual. The critic articulates the meaning that a body of work assumes in the specific pragmatic context of the critical act. Piotrowski's monograph does not "reveal" the eternal significance of contextual art; it produces a specific, historically situated reading valid in the context of 2015 Poland — but subject to "outdating" by future contexts.

The awareness that one's own critical practice is subject to the same contextual determination as the art it addresses — that the critic, no less than the artist, is a "neo-pragmatic tourist" — may be contextual art's most profound lesson for the humanities. It leads not to relativism (remember Thesis 12) but to responsibility: the responsibility to specify the context from which one speaks, to acknowledge the situatedness of one's claims, and to remain open to revision.

This report itself is no exception. It was produced in a specific context — March 2026, by a researcher using specific tools (NotebookLM, digital archives, primary documents), drawing on specific scholarly traditions, and shaped by specific conversations with persons who knew the artist. A different researcher, working from different archives, in a different institutional and personal context, would produce a different — equally valid — reading. "One and only one expression is true in the given pragmatic context." This expression, too, is true only here and now.

Appendix A: The 12 Theses of Contextual Art (1976) — Complete Text

First published in Art as Contextual Art, Galerie S:t Petri, Lund, Sweden, August 1976.

1. There are no objects without a meaning as there are no meanings without objects. One and the same object may have different meanings in different codes, one and the same meaning may be ascribed to different objects. All this leads to two types of multi-meaning: (i) the multi-meaning of objects which have the same meaning, (ii) the multi-meaning of meanings which have the same object. In a particular context only one meaning is accepted as the true one. The criterion of choice is the criterion of truth.

2. If different social groups ascribe different meanings to one and the same object and one of those groups commands sufficient authority to enforce its viewpoint on others, the problem arises of finding a suitable criterion of choice. This leads to the many definitions of art (everything may be art). Since civilization is changing extremely fast today, there is little time for new meanings of objects to crystallize. In effect the objects of art assume different meanings (everything may be accepted as art).

3. In our practical activities we create objects with a given meaning (concepts). This is how conventional art works. In our cognitive activities we create meanings (concepts) possessing given objects. This is how conceptual art works. Both of these operations have a feedback relation. The reality that we shape depends on our concepts of reality, our concepts of reality depend on the reality that we create. Therefore the choice of criterion defines the reality that we shape.

4. The changes that occur in that reality make the existing meanings no longer valid; social practice becomes the ultimate criterion. The fact that certain meanings lose their validity affects our life-styles, results in a loss of privileges that have been gained by one group in favour of another. There is therefore a constant trend towards upholding meanings which have lost their validity. Because of the diversified range of meanings in the definition systems, there is the possibility of constructing apparent meanings of reality, and in consequence, of producing an apparent reality.

5. The outdating of meanings is a constant process — proceeding all the faster the quicker civilization changes. Contextual art proposes a sign, the criterion of truth of which, defined by the pragmatic context, changes incessantly. Object "O" assumes the meaning "m" in time "t," place "p," situation "s," in relation to the person/persons "x" then and only then. A change of any of those elements outdates the previous meaning.

6. The operation of contextual art is therefore a steady rejection of canons used to arrest the outdating of meanings.

7. Contextual art opposes the stabilization of the objects of art, since the lasting nature of an object extends into its meaning. Contextual art opposes the stabilization of meanings, since the lasting nature of meanings leads to the production of outdated objects. It therefore rejects the definitions of art.

8. In the relation linking meaning and the object to which it is applied, in the case of conventional art both components remain stable. Since Duchamp's time, the second of these components has been set into motion (every object may mean an art). In contextual art both components change (also each object may have the meaning of an "art" and not be an art). The aim of art is the relativization of the entire area of meanings and of objects.

9. Art as contextual art is concerned with the relation itself (the readiness to reup the meaning of the object in a defined pragmatic context).

10. The expressions of contextual art are not expressions which would be acceptable to a group since the reality construed by the artist is something different than that which is received. (It changes all the time).

11. Art as contextual art has no connection with science. Science has to define (immobilize) an object by giving its meaning. It is beyond the sphere of formal logic. The world in which it exists is not an area of formalized axioms, but rules which become constantly outdated and which attempts to preserve the changing reality.

12. Art as contextual art is opposed to multi-meaning and also to relativism. One and only one expression is true in the given pragmatic context. Such expression is expressed with assertion.

"The definition of art, calling something an object of art, a work of art, depends on a set of concepts whose rules are known and accepted by the social group accepting the given phenomenon as art."

Pewne wyjaśnienie.

Skąd wziął mi się ten tytuł: anomia? Nie jest to zbyt popularne słowo. Wprowadził je do socjologii Durkheim na oznaczenie stanu społeczeństwa, gdy ogólnie przyjęte normy, wartości, pojęcia — cały przez wszystkich zrozumiały obraz rzeczywistości przestaje funkcjonować.

About This Report

This study was compiled in March 2026 using primary sources from the __SWIDZINSKI archive (50+ files), 13 original texts by Świdziński (from swidzinski.art.pl, all saved as .txt), 3 interviews (Sztuka i Filozofia 2006, Atlas Sztuki 2004, Fototapeta 2001), eyewitness testimony (TR Warszawa 2014), scholarly criticism (Piotrowski, Ronduda, Guzek, Łukasiewicz, Libera, Przyłuski), and ~100 indexed sources across two NotebookLM collections.

Note on Libera's biographical claims: The allegations regarding wartime service and postwar security service collaboration, published in Przekrój (2020), have not been independently verified by IPN historians or other neutral experts. They are presented in this report with appropriate scholarly caveats. SB archival materials require specialist interpretation and should not be accepted at face value.
